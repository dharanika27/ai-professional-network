# Interview Preparation Guide

## The STAR Method for Behavioral Questions

Behavioral interviews are based on the premise that past behavior predicts future performance. The STAR method (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the gold standard framework for answering behavioral questions.

**Situation:** Set the scene with necessary context. Keep it brief — one to two sentences. Provide just enough background for the interviewer to understand the scenario without extensive explanation.

**Task:** Describe your specific responsibility or challenge. What were you personally accountable for? Clarify your role versus the team's collective responsibility.

**Action:** This is the most important part. Describe in detail what YOU specifically did — not what "we" did. Use "I" statements. Walk through your decision-making process. What options did you consider? Why did you choose this approach? What steps did you execute?

**Result:** Quantify the outcome. What changed because of your actions? Use specific numbers: percentage improvements, cost savings, time reductions, revenue generated. If the project is still ongoing, share the interim results. Always address what you learned if the result was negative.

STAR answers should be 2-4 minutes long. Practice until you can deliver them fluidly without memorization — sounding rehearsed is almost as bad as being unprepared.

## Common Behavioral Question Categories

Prepare 3-5 STAR stories that can flex across multiple question types. The same achievement can answer different questions depending on which element you emphasize.

**Leadership and influence:**
- "Tell me about a time you led a project without formal authority."
- "Describe a situation where you had to convince skeptical stakeholders."
- "Give an example of mentoring someone and the impact it had."

**Problem-solving and technical judgment:**
- "Tell me about the most complex technical problem you've solved."
- "Describe a time you had to make a decision with incomplete information."
- "Give an example of when you identified a risk before it became a problem."

**Conflict and collaboration:**
- "Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager."
- "Describe a situation where you had to work with a difficult colleague."
- "Give an example of a time your team was failing to meet a deadline."

**Failure and learning:**
- "Tell me about your biggest professional failure."
- "Describe a time you missed a target or made a serious mistake."
- "Give an example of feedback you received that changed how you work."

## Technical Interview Preparation

For technical roles, preparation is domain-specific but follows predictable patterns.

**Software engineering:** Practice data structures and algorithms on LeetCode, HackerRank, or similar platforms. Focus on arrays, hash maps, trees, graphs, dynamic programming, and system design. Review Big-O complexity for common operations. For senior roles, system design questions (design Twitter, design a URL shortener) matter as much as coding problems.

**Data and analytics:** Be prepared to write SQL queries including window functions, CTEs, and aggregations. Practice explaining your approach to ambiguous data problems. Know your metrics: precision vs. recall, statistical significance, A/B test design.

**Product and business roles:** Practice case frameworks: market sizing, root cause analysis, product improvement. Know how to prioritize a feature backlog (ICE scoring, RICE, Kano model). Be ready to discuss metrics: DAU/MAU, retention cohorts, conversion funnels, LTV:CAC ratios.

## Company Research Checklist

Arrive at every interview knowing more about the company than 95% of candidates. This turns routine questions into differentiated conversations.

Before the interview, research:
- Recent earnings calls, press releases, or blog posts about company direction
- The interviewer's LinkedIn profile, publications, or conference talks
- The product you'd be working on — sign up for a trial if possible
- Competitors and the competitive landscape
- Recent news (fundraising, acquisitions, layoffs, leadership changes)
- The company's stated mission, values, and culture

Use this research to customize your answers: "I noticed from your Q3 earnings call that reducing infrastructure costs is a priority — at my current role, I reduced cloud spend by 35% by rightsizing our Kubernetes clusters."

## Questions to Ask the Interviewer

Asking strong questions signals genuine interest, intellectual engagement, and that you've done your homework. Prepare 5-7 questions so you always have one that hasn't already been answered.

High-signal questions:
- "What does success look like for this role in the first 90 days?"
- "What's the biggest challenge the team is facing right now?"
- "How does the team make prioritization decisions when there are competing demands?"
- "What do you wish you had known before joining this company?"
- "How has the engineering culture evolved over the past year?"

Avoid questions about salary, PTO, and benefits until you have an offer. These can be asked of a recruiter, not your hiring manager.

## Follow-Up Thank-You Notes

Send a thank-you email within 24 hours of each interview. This is an underutilized differentiator — fewer than 20% of candidates send them, but they meaningfully influence hiring decisions.

Structure: Express genuine appreciation for their time → reference one specific thing from your conversation → reinforce your fit with a brief reminder of a relevant strength → express continued enthusiasm for the role.

Keep it under 150 words. Send separate notes to each interviewer with different specific references to your conversation. Generic templates forwarded to everyone are easily spotted and undermine the gesture.
